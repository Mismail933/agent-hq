TITLE: The Self-Taught Clockmaker Who Beat Britain's Scientists
SPINE: once = a sailor out of sight of land could not tell how far east or west he was; every_day = captains guessed, and ships ran onto rocks;
one_day = in 1707 a fleet sailed home through fog and smashed into the rocks off the Isles of Scilly; because1 = so Parliament offered a fortune to
whoever could solve it; because2 = a clockmaker realised time itself could give the answer, if a clock could survive the sea; finally = his watch lost five seconds in eighty-one days;
question_answered_in_scene = 11
1 [first] "How did a self-taught clockmaker beat the finest scientists in Britain?" | SHOWS: the Atlantic on a map, a small ship setting out | STEP: This is a puzzle about the sea, and one man who solved it.
2 [because] "Because in 1707, a British fleet sailed home through fog and smashed into the rocks near the Isles of Scilly." | SHOWS: the English coast on a map, a red cross on the rocks | STEP: Sailors were dying within sight of home.
3 [because] "The captains had good maps, but they could not tell how far east or west they were." | SHOWS: a ship alone on open sea, a question mark over the map | STEP: Nobody could find longitude, the east-west position, at sea.
4 [so] "So in 1714, Parliament offered a fortune to anyone who could solve it." | SHOWS: a bag of gold coins and a scroll on a desk | STEP: There was a huge prize for the answer.
5 [but] "But the experts looked to the stars, and stars are very hard to use on a ship that never stops rolling." | SHOWS: a ship tilting under a starry sky, a scientist wobbling at a telescope | STEP: The famous scientists' idea did not work at sea.
6 [but] "John Harrison had a simpler idea: the Earth turns fifteen degrees every hour, so a clock can tell you where you are." | SHOWS: a globe turning slowly with a clock beside it | STEP: The gap between home time and local time gives your east-west position.
7 [so] "If your clock still shows home time, and the sun says it is noon, then the gap between the two is your distance from home." | SHOWS: a clock at home time, the sun overhead, an arrow measuring the gap | STEP: How the method works, step by step.
8 [but] "But no clock could keep good time on a ship that rocked, froze and baked for months." | SHOWS: a pendulum clock swinging wildly on a deck | STEP: The hard part was building a clock that survives the sea.
9 [so] "So Harrison spent nearly thirty years building better and better ones, until he made a watch the size of a big pocket watch." | SHOWS: Harrison at a workbench holding a round brass watch | STEP: He kept improving his clocks.
10 [therefore] "In 1761 his watch crossed the Atlantic, and lost only five seconds in eighty-one days." | SHOWS: a ship reaching an island, the watch face with a tiny gap marked | STEP: It worked, with an error small enough to save ships.
11 [therefore] "That is how a self-taught clockmaker solved the great puzzle of the sea, and why every ship after him knew where it was." | SHOWS: the narrator beside a ship's wheel with a clock on the deck | STEP: Harrison's clock answered the question.
</example>

TITLE: How a Sun Symbol Unlocked Ancient Egypt
SPINE: once = nobody alive could read Egyptian hieroglyphs; every_day = scholars stared at carved walls and guessed; one_day = in 1799 French soldiers found a stone with the same message in three scripts;
because1 = so one scholar could finally compare an unknown script with a known one; because2 = he knew Coptic, a later form of the Egyptian language; finally = a sun symbol turned out to be a sound, and a king's name appeared;
question_answered_in_scene = 10
1 [first] "How do you read a language that nobody has understood for fourteen hundred years?" | SHOWS: a wall of carved symbols in a lamplit room | STEP: Egypt's writing had been a mystery for centuries.
2 [because] "Because in 1799, French soldiers digging near the town of Rosetta found a stone with the same message written three times." | SHOWS: a dark stone slab being lifted out of the sand | STEP: A key to the puzzle had been found.
3 [so] "One version was in Greek, which scholars could read, so they knew the other two must say the same thing." | SHOWS: three bands of writing on the stone, the Greek one glowing | STEP: The Greek could unlock the hieroglyphs.
4 [but] "But nobody knew whether those little pictures stood for ideas, or for sounds." | SHOWS: a row of birds, eyes and waves with question marks above them | STEP: The big question: pictures, or sounds?
5 [so] "A young Frenchman named Jean-François Champollion worked on it for years, and he already knew Coptic, a later form of the Egyptian language." | SHOWS: Champollion at a desk piled with papers | STEP: He had a special tool: Coptic.
6 [so] "He studied the names of kings, because those were written inside oval frames called cartouches." | SHOWS: an oval frame around a few symbols, lit up | STEP: Royal names are easy to spot.
7 [so] "One cartouche held a sun and a few small marks. The Coptic word for sun sounds like Ra." | SHOWS: the sun symbol glowing, the sound RA appearing beside it | STEP: A picture of the sun stood for a sound, Ra.
8 [therefore] "Add the sounds of the other marks, and they spelled Ramesses, a pharaoh he knew from history." | SHOWS: the marks turning into letters that build the name RAMESSES | STEP: The signs were sounds that spell a name he already knew.
9 [therefore] "So the signs could stand for sounds as well as ideas, and the whole language could be read." | SHOWS: signs on a wall turning into readable lines one by one | STEP: Hieroglyphs work like pictures plus an alphabet.
10 [therefore] "That is how a stone and a sun symbol let us hear the voices of ancient Egypt again." | SHOWS: the stone, then a temple wall glowing with readable text | STEP: Reading hieroglyphs became possible.
</example>

TITLE: The Engineer Who Saved London From Its Own Smell
SPINE: once = London poured its sewage straight into the river it drank from; every_day = the water carried disease and the river grew fouler; one_day = in the hot summer of 1858 the smell became unbearable even in Parliament;
because1 = so Parliament finally gave the job to an engineer; because2 = he planned to catch the sewage before it reached the river and carry it away downhill; finally = the river cleared and his tunnels still work;
question_answered_in_scene = 10
1 [first] "How did one engineer rescue a city from a stink so bad that Parliament nearly ran away from it?" | SHOWS: the river and Parliament under a hot sun, wet sheets hanging in the windows | STEP: A whole city faced an unbearable crisis.
2 [because] "Because in the hot summer of 1858, London's river was full of everything the city had thrown into it." | SHOWS: a brown river with a sewer pipe pouring into it | STEP: Sewage went straight into the river that people drank from.
3 [so] "The smell was so bad that politicians hung sheets soaked in chemicals across the windows of Parliament." | SHOWS: a window with a dripping white sheet | STEP: The problem finally reached the people in power.
4 [so] "So they gave the job to an engineer named Joseph Bazalgette." | SHOWS: the narrator handing rolled plans to a man in a top hat | STEP: Bazalgette took charge.
5 [but] "But you cannot simply move a river of sewage, so he planned to catch it before it ever reached the Thames." | SHOWS: a city map with pipes crossing it and leading away | STEP: His idea: intercept the sewage.
6 [so] "He built great sloping tunnels under the streets, and gravity carried everything east, away from the city." | SHOWS: a cross-section of a street with a brick tunnel running gently downhill | STEP: How it works: gravity does the carrying.
7 [but] "But he knew London would keep growing, so he made every tunnel much bigger than it needed to be." | SHOWS: a small pipe beside a bigger pipe, the bigger one marked as the plan | STEP: He built for the future.
8 [so] "It took more than three hundred million bricks and about six years." | SHOWS: bricks piling up into an arched tunnel | STEP: The work was enormous.
9 [therefore] "When it was done, the sewage flowed out to sea, and the river slowly began to clear." | SHOWS: the river turning from brown to blue | STEP: It worked.
10 [therefore] "That is how one engineer, some bricks and a little gravity cleaned up a capital, and much of his system still works today." | SHOWS: a modern city above, the old brick tunnel still running below | STEP: The hook is answered, and the result lasted.
</example>
